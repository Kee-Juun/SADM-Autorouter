# Out of Spec Dialogue Voice Guide

## Intent

Dialogue should feel like people talking before it feels like fantasy writing.
This guide takes broad craft lessons from character-led adventure and mystery
fiction without copying any author's phrasing or signature style.

The approved opening conversation is the primary tone reference. Prefer its
plain questions, small misunderstandings, casual teasing, and gradual discovery
over polished banter or quotable speeches.

## Knowledge Rule

The Last Clerk wakes with no usable knowledge of the setting. He does not know
what an Archive is, why a room can speak, what a seal or relic does, what kind
of creature confronts him, or which impossible events are considered ordinary.
Write him as a normal person encountering each fact for the first time.

- He names what he can physically see before accepting Mara's terminology.
- He asks the plain question a real newcomer would ask, including "What is
  that?", "Is it talking?", and "Why would I do that?"
- He cannot make informed jokes about a rule before someone explains or
  demonstrates that rule.
- His humor comes from the mismatch between ordinary expectations and what is
  happening, not from familiarity with fantasy or game conventions.
- Once he learns something on screen, later dialogue may use that vocabulary.
  Knowledge is cumulative, never retroactive.

Mara knows the world, but she remembers that he does not. She introduces one
strange fact at a time, responds to his actual question, and uses concrete
instructions before institutional terminology.

## Scene Rule

Before writing a line, answer three questions:

1. What does the speaker want in the next thirty seconds?
2. What are they unwilling to say directly?
3. What changes because they spoke?

If a line only explains lore, move the information into an object, action,
interruption, or disagreement. Characters are not glossary entries.

## The Last Clerk

- Notices concrete details before naming themes.
- Reacts as an ordinary confused person placed inside a magical institution;
  he is not genre-aware and does not know the world's rules in advance.
- Is confused in the same order a real person would be: safety, place, people,
  strange objects, then the larger mystery.
- Asks the question a player would naturally ask in that moment, even when the
  question sounds obvious.
- Uses dry humor to steady himself, not to perform for an audience.
- Pushes when Mara avoids a direct answer, but does not become cruel.
- Speaks in short, ordinary sentences and sometimes misunderstands Mara.
- Becomes quieter, not more eloquent, when genuinely frightened.

Avoid making every line a punchline. His humor works because plain sincerity
and uncertainty surround it.

## Mara Venn

- Solves the immediate problem first: sit down, step back, bring the Lens.
- Sounds like a capable coworker, not an oracle or a lecturer.
- Knows the Archive intimately but does not recite it like a tour guide.
- Deflects personal questions, then corrects herself when the deflection hurts.
- Shows affection through attention, preparation, and remembered details.
- Uses precise formal language when afraid; this should be noticeable because
  her ordinary speech is direct and conversational.
- Can say "I don't know," "I was wrong," and "I am afraid" without decorating
  the admission.
- Lightly teases the Clerk, but does not always have a prepared comeback.

Mara may be witty, but she should not turn every instruction into an Archive
proverb or every vulnerable moment into a polished speech.

## Their Relationship

- Early scenes: wary cooperation, quick observation, accidental familiarity.
- Middle scenes: private callbacks, concern disguised as practical care, small
  disagreements about evidence and risk.
- Late scenes: fewer jokes, cleaner honesty, and pauses where a joke would once
  have been used as cover.
- Attraction remains light and implicit. Trust is the primary relationship arc.
- Neither character should always "win" the exchange.

## Rhythm

- Prefer two to four short turns over one explanatory paragraph.
- Let characters ask follow-up questions before introducing a new proper noun.
- Let answers be partial. Follow-up questions create natural exposition.
- Interrupt abstractions with something physical in the room.
- Vary cadence: statement, question, correction, silence.
- Permit small verbal imperfections: a restart, a clipped answer, a thought the
  speaker chooses not to finish.
- Do not polish every sentence into a maxim, metaphor, or clever quotation.

## Humor

Humor must do at least one job:

- reveal fear;
- test trust;
- ease another person's embarrassment;
- expose an absurd institution;
- recall a shared moment.

Remove jokes that merely announce that the script knows it is clever. One good
exchange lands better than three consecutive quips.

## Exposition Check

Rewrite a line when it:

- states the theme before the player has experienced it;
- contains more than one new proper noun;
- sounds equally plausible from either Mara or the Clerk;
- explains information both characters already know;
- ends with a polished metaphor during immediate danger;
- repeats a gameplay instruction already visible in the interface.

## Quick Example Pattern

Instead of a single lore speech:

> Mara explains the mechanism, its history, and its moral meaning.

Use an exchange:

> Mara gives the action the Clerk must take. The Clerk notices one troubling
> physical detail. Mara answers too quickly. He catches it. She admits only the
> part they need now. The unanswered part becomes a reason to keep playing.
